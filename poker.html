Hand rankings

Your hand is the best five cards you can make from your two hole cards and the five community cards. The odds below are for seven cards, which is what you actually see by the river. (The evaluator on this page reproduces every one of them within sampling error over two million random deals.)

How a hand plays out

The two players left of the dealer button post the small blind (10) and big blind (20). Everyone gets two cards, then there are four betting rounds: preflop, the flop (three shared cards), the turn (one more) and the river (the last). On each round you can fold, check or call, or bet and raise.

Position is power. The button acts last on every street after the flop, so it sees what everyone else does first. That’s why the opening ranges widen from 17% of hands under the gun to 45% on the button.

Pot odds: when a call pays

To call, you need to win often enough to cover the price. Equity needed = call ÷ (pot after your call). Facing a half-pot bet, you risk 1 to win 2, so you need 25%. The coach shows this line on the equity bar so you can see at a glance whether you clear it.

Outs and the rule of 2 and 4

An out is an unseen card that turns your hand into a winner. Quick estimate: multiply outs by 4 on the flop (two cards to come) or 2 on the turn. Here’s how that shortcut compares with the exact odds:

DrawOutsFlop → riverRule of 4Turn → riverRule of 2
The rule of 4 overshoots as outs grow — with 15 outs it says 60% when the truth is 54%. The coach counts your outs and shows both numbers.

Preflop

  • Raise, don’t limp. If a hand is good enough to play first in, open to 2.5–3 big blinds. Raising can win the blinds right away; limping invites everyone in.
  • Tighten up facing a raise. Re-raise (3-bet) your best hands — roughly QQ+ and AK — call with strong hands that play well after the flop, fold the rest.
  • Defend the big blind wider. You already have 20 in the pot, so a min-raise gives you a great price.
  • Suited and connected hands make straights and flushes; big offsuit aces make top pair with a good kicker. Weak offsuit hands like K7o or Q5o mostly make second-best hands.

The four buckets

Every time it’s your turn, sort your hand into one of four buckets based on what your opponents are likely to hold. The bucket tells you the play, and the coach labels every decision this way.

Value
Ahead of most hands that will call. Bet or raise to get paid.
Top pair with a good kicker, two pair, sets · preflop: QQ+, AK
2
Showdown value
Beats their bluffs, loses to their value. Check, and call when the pot odds say so.
Middle pair, weak top pair, a draw in a multiway pot · preflop: hands good enough to call a raise
3
Bluffs & semi-bluffs
Can’t win a showdown as it stands, but can win when they fold, or improve. Bet or raise the same size as bucket 1.
Flush and straight draws, a c-bet with air, a missed draw when they check the river · preflop: A5s–A2s as 3-bets
4
Give up
Nothing to show down, no draw, and nobody who’ll fold. Check and fold.
Air against a calling station or three opponents · preflop: anything outside the chart

Why play 1s and 3s the same? If you only bet your good hands, opponents fold everything except better hands and you never get paid. Mix in bluffs at the same size and they can’t tell which one you have. How many bluffs? With a bet of B into a pot of P, a balanced betting range is B ÷ (P + 2B) bluffs: about 1 bluff for every 2 value bets with a pot-sized bet, 1 for every 3 with a half-pot bet.

Against a calling station, bucket 3 disappears: stations don’t fold, so your bluffs become give-ups and your thin value bets get paid instead.

What maximizes profit

Poker has two kinds of “optimal.” A game-theory-optimal (GTO) strategy is an equilibrium: in a two-player game it guarantees you at least break even against anyone, however well they play. Computers have gotten there. Cepheus essentially solved heads-up limit hold’em in 2015, and Libratus beat four top professionals at heads-up no-limit by 14.7 big blinds per 100 hands over 120,000 hands in 2017. A maximally exploitative strategy aims at one opponent’s leaks instead. It wins more against weak players, but a strong player can turn it against you.

Most pros start from the GTO baseline and deviate when they spot a leak. The coach’s Balanced setting is that baseline; Max profit adds the reads.

Heads-up
The four buckets were made for this. Ranges get wide — solvers open around 80–90% of hands from the button and defend 60–70% from the big blind — and a balanced bet carries bluffs in a fixed ratio to value.
Multiway
A bluff has to get through everyone, so betting gets honest and value-heavy and bucket 3 shrinks to strong draws. Six-player poker also has no equilibrium guarantee: Pluribus beat pros in 2019 with self-play and search instead.
Weak opponents
Exploiting beats balance. Against stations, value-bet bigger and thinner and stop bluffing. Against nits, steal and bluff more and respect their bets. Against maniacs, tighten up and let them bluff into you.

After the flop

Value bet
When you’re ahead of most hands that would call, bet. Half to two-thirds of the pot gets called by worse hands and charges draws.
Continuation bet
If you raised preflop, a small bet on the flop often takes the pot — most flops miss most hands. Best heads-up and on dry boards.
Semi-bluff
Betting with a draw wins two ways: they fold now, or you hit. Flush and open-ended straight draws are the classic candidates.
Bluff sparingly
A pot-sized river bet only needs to work a third of the time. Bluff against thinking players on scary boards — never against a calling station.
Pot control
With a medium hand (middle pair, weak top pair) checking keeps the pot small and lets you get to showdown cheaply.
Believe big raises
A check-raise or a raise on the turn usually means two pair or better. Most players, including these AIs, don’t bluff-raise often.

The table

Five personalities rotate through the seats. They play from the same rules the coach explains, bent by style: how many hands they play, how often they bet and raise, how much they bluff, and how hard they are to shake off a pot. When someone busts, a new player takes the seat. Heads-up, you pick your opponent.

How the coach works

On your turn the coach deals out the rest of the hand thousands of times. Each opponent gets random hole cards drawn from a range built from their actions: a preflop raise narrows it according to the raiser’s seat and style (a cutoff open is about the top 28% at six-max; heads-up the button opens most hands), a 3-bet narrows it much further, and every bet after the flop tightens it again. Your equity is your share of the pots across all those runouts.

Because it’s a simulation, it comes with a 95% confidence interval, ±1.96 √(p(1−p)/n). It narrows as the simulation count climbs — watch it tighten in the coach panel.

Then it picks a bucket. Preflop, that comes from solver-style ranges for the table size: value hands raise, some hands raise as steals or 3-bet bluffs, good-not-great hands call, the rest fold. After the flop it uses your equity against a fair share of the pot (50% heads-up, 33% three-way), your outs, the board, whether you raised preflop, and who you’re up against. Facing a bet, bucket 2 hands call only when your equity beats the pot odds.

With Max profit on, it also reads opponent types: it steals from nits, value-bets thinner and bigger into calling stations and never bluffs them, and gives maniacs room to bluff. Balanced treats everyone as a solid regular. The simulator decided which reads to keep: a read stays only if it beat the balanced baseline on paired hands.

The hand evaluator is bitmask-based and was checked against a brute-force evaluator on 60,000 random showdowns with zero disagreements.

Reading your win rate

Win rate is measured in big blinds per 100 hands (bb/100). A strong player might win 5–10 bb/100, but a single session swings by 100 bb or more. That’s why the Session panel shows a 95% confidence interval: after 200 hands it is still very wide. Judge your play by your decisions, not your results.
